Abstract—This letter proposes an ultrawideband 1-bit sus-
pended reconﬁgurable intelligent surface (RIS) designed to en-
hance the signal coverage of wireless communication networks.
The RIS unit cell comprises two metal gratings connected via a
PIN diode, a dielectric-air-dielectric interlayer, and a metal reﬂec-
tive layer. The RIS achieves a stable reﬂection phase difference
of 180° ± 30° between the ON and OFF states of the PIN diode
within the ultrawideband range of 2.75 GHz to 6 GHz. This range
includes the 3.4 GHz and 4.9 GHz bands for 5G communication, the
5.2 GHz band for indoor wireless communication, and the 5.8 GHz
band for Wi-Fi and Bluetooth signals. A prototype, consisting of
20 × 20 unit cells, was fabricated and measured, demonstrating
two-dimensional wide-angle beam scanning of ± 60° within the
working frequency range. Furthermore, a RIS-assisted wireless
communication system was constructed, conﬁrming that deploying
RIS can signiﬁcantly enhance communication quality in targeted
areas by transmitting and receiving image information at differ-
ent frequencies. The proposed RIS holds substantial potential for
RIS-assisted wireless communication systems.

I. INTRODUCTION

ETASURFACES, as two-dimensional equivalents of
electromagnetic metamaterials, have garnered signiﬁ-
cant attention in recent years due to their ability to control the
phase, amplitude, and polarization of electromagnetic waves [1].
Generally, the function of passive metasurfaces is ﬁxed after
fabrication, limiting their ability to ﬂexibly manipulate electro-
magneticwaves[2].Toachievedynamicreal-timemanipulation,
reconﬁgurable intelligent surfaces (RISs) incorporating tunable
components [3] and tunable materials [4] have been extensively
studied. This advancement has led to many attractive applica-
tions, including stealth technology [5], imaging [6], wireless
communication systems [7], and radar systems [8].
With the growing demand for wireless communication, RISs
have emerged as an appealing solution for enhancing indoor
wireless communication [9]. By dynamically guiding the re-
ﬂected beam, RISs can cover signal blind spots and improve

II. DESIGN OF THE ULTRAWIDEBAND SUSPENDED RIS
The schematic of the designed suspended RIS unit cell is
shown in Fig. 2. It consists of two metal gratings connected
through a PIN diode, a dielectric-air-dielectric interlayer, and a
metal reﬂective layer. The pattern layer is suspended above the
bottom reﬂective layer through an air gap to achieve wideband
characteristics. The right metal gratings are connected to the
bias lines on the back of the ﬁrst dielectric layer through vias
to allow independent control of the PIN diodes. The inﬂuence
of bias lines on the electromagnetic response of the unit cell
has been considered in the simulation. The length of the left
gratings equals the unit cell period, so the left gratings of the
whole column are connected as the common ground of the PIN
diodes. The unit cell period is p = 18 mm, approximately equal
to 0.26λ at the center frequency. The thickness of the dielectric
layers and the air layer are h1(2) = 3 mm and hair = 5.5 mm,
respectively. The relative permittivity and loss tangent of the
dielectric substrate are 2.5 and 0.001, respectively. The other
detailed parameters are as follows: w1(2) = 3.5 mm, l1 = 18
mm, l2 = 17.5 mm, g1 = 4.4 mm, g2 = 1.3 mm, and w3 =
1 mm. The PIN diode used in the unit cell is the Skyworks
SMP1320-079LF, and its equivalent circuit is given in Fig. 2(c),
where RON = 0.5 Ω, L on = 0.7 nH, C = 0.24 pF, R off = 0.4
MΩ, and LOFF = 0.5 nH.
The unit cell was simulated using periodic boundaries and a
Floquet port in CST Microwave Studio. The reﬂection amplitude
and phase response of the unit cell between the ON and OFF states
of the PIN diode are shown in Fig. 3. The simulated results
indicate that the unit cell has distinct resonant frequencies in the
ON and OFF states of the PIN diode, with a maximum loss of 0.33
dB within the frequency band. The widely separated resonant

To elucidate the mechanism of the ultrawideband phase re-
sponse of the RIS, the electric ﬁeld distribution and the surface
current distribution of the unit cell in the two states of the
PIN diode at frequencies of 3.4 GHz, 4.9 GHz, and 5.8 GHz
were simulated, as shown in Fig. 4. The two metal gratings
are short-circuited in the ON state, while a capacitance exists
between the two metal gratings in the OFF state. In the ON state,
the two gratings form an electric dipole to generate electric
resonance, whereas in the OFF state, it is equivalent to a double
electric dipole resonance. These different resonant modes lead to
the generation of an abrupt phase change. Moreover, the surface
currentsareinvertedinbothstates,resultinginaphasedifference
of 180°.

III. FABRICATION AND MEASUREMENT OF THE
ULTRAWIDEBAND SUSPENDED RIS
To verify the performance of the designed RIS, a prototype
was fabricated using printed circuit board technology, as shown
in Fig. 6(a). The rectangular array consists of 20 × 20 unit
cells measuring 400 mm × 400 mm. The dielectric layers are
suspended and ﬁxed by plastic studs at the four corners, forming
a dielectric-air-dielectric interlayer. Additionally, 13 nH choke
inductors (Murata LQP03HQ13NH02D) are loaded on the dc
bias lines to isolate the RF signal. Since the state of the PIN
diode of each unit cell needs to be controlled independently, a
ﬁeld-programmable gate array (FPGA) provides different bias
voltages. It is worth noting that the total power consumption of
the RIS system in operation is 4.8 W to 8.2 W.
The fabricated prototype was measured using the reﬂectivity
experimental system, as depicted in Fig. 6(b) and (c). The exper-
imental system consists of a vector network analyzer, two arched
arms loaded with a transmitting horn antenna and a receiving
horn antenna, and arched arm rotation control equipment. The
left horn antenna radiates a quasi-plane wave as excitation,
while the right horn antenna receives the reﬂected wave from
the proposed RIS. The right arm rotates to scan and record the
reﬂection coefﬁcients for different angles to build the radiation
patterns. Additionally, absorbent materials were laid on the
ground and pointed by the horn antennas to absorb scattered
waves.

By setting all PIN diodes to either the ON or OFF state, the
amplitude response and phase response were measured in both
states, as shown in Fig. 7. Due to limitations of the experimental
system, the incident angle of the transmitting antenna is set
to 4° to approximate normal incidence, while the receiving
antenna is placed symmetrically on the opposite side of the
normal plane. The measured results show that the fabricated
RIS meets the ultrawideband 1-bit phase response within 3 GHz
to 6.25 GHz, with a relative bandwidth of 70.3% and a maximum
loss within the frequency band of 0.57 dB. It can be observed
that the amplitude response slightly decreases compared to the
simulation, and the phase response has a slight deviation. The
primary reasons for the deviation include manufacturing errors
of the prototype, measurement errors of the system, and the
bendingoftheRISplaneundergravityduetoinsufﬁcientrigidity
and lack of plastic studs supporting the top layer at the center.
In subsequent experiments, we measured the dynamic real-
time beam deﬂection performance of the RIS prototype. Due
to the limitations of the experimental system, we provided
the one-dimensional beam scanning results of the RIS in the
xoz-plane. By setting different phase codes into the RIS, the
radiation patterns of the beams with different deﬂection angles
at 3.4 GHz, 4.9 GHz, and 5.8 GHz were obtained, as shown in
Fig. 8. The main beams all point to speciﬁc directions, verifying
the correctness of the theoretical design. As the beam deﬂection
angle increases, the beam deﬂection performance deteriorates.

IV. RIS-ASSISTED WIRELESS COMMUNICATION SYSTEM
In this section, we constructed a RIS-assisted intelligent wire-
lesscommunicationsystemandveriﬁedthattheRIScanenhance
the quality of wireless communication. The system consists
of a transmitting horn antenna, a receiving horn antenna, a
software-deﬁned radio device (ANTSDR-E310), a RIS, and host
computers, as shown in Fig. 9. The transmitting horn antenna
is placed 1.2 m from the RIS, with the incident wave direction
alignedalongthenormaldirectionoftheRIS.Thereceivinghorn
antenna is positioned at a 45° angle to the normal of the RIS. By
controlling the FPGA module through another host computer,
the phase coding data is written in real time to deﬂect the incident
wave in the desired direction. Thus, the blind area coverage
of the wireless communication network is enhanced, and the
communication quality in the required area is improved. The
original image information can be obtained by demodulating
the received signal.
For veriﬁcation, we selected the 3.4 GHz and 4.9 GHz bands
of 5G communication, as well as the 5.8 GHz band of Wi-Fi
and Bluetooth signals, to transmit and receive different image
information. Table I illustrates the original images sent at dif-
ferent frequencies using phase shift keying (de)modulation, the
images received with the RIS switched OFF and reﬂecting the
incident wave in the normal direction, and the images received
after the RIS deﬂects the incident wave toward the receiving
horn antenna. It can be observed that image transmission errors
occur when the RIS is not functioning, resulting in image dis-
tortion. Conversely, the communication quality at the speciﬁc
location is signiﬁcantly improved when the RIS is operational,
verifying the RIS’s effectiveness in enhancing indoor wireless
communication. The relatively good images received when the
RIS is switched OFF are due to the side lobes of the transmitting
antenna and scattered waves contributing to the propagation
link, allowing part of the wireless signal to be received by the
receiving antenna.

V. CONCLUSION
In this letter, a 1-bit suspended RIS with an ultrawideband
phase response was designed, fabricated, and measured. The
measured results show that the RIS can achieve dynamic beam
deﬂection with a scanning range of ± 60° within the working
frequency. The measured results are in good agreement with the
simulation results, verifying the performance of the proposed
RIS. Moreover, a RIS-assisted wireless communication system
was constructed for experimental veriﬁcation. The feasibility
and effectiveness of RIS for enhancing wireless communica-
tion quality have been demonstrated. The proposed RIS of-
fers promising application prospects in the new generation of
intelligent wireless communication systems.
